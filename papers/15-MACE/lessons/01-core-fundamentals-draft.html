Core fundamentals

MACE turns nearby atomic positions and elements into one energy contribution per atom. Its internal quantities preserve geometric behavior, and one layer can combine several neighbors.

Part 1

The complete local pass

One receiving atom moves through a fixed sequence of operations. Each operation processes many atoms or edges together, while the sequence itself remains serial.

Atomistic graph

A graph embedded in three-dimensional space. Nodes represent atoms; directed edges connect atoms that lie within a chosen distance.

Local environment

The receiving atom and the neighboring atoms connected to it inside a chosen maximum distance.

Radial basis and multilayer perceptron (MLP)

The basis expands one interatomic distance into several scalar edge features. The MLP maps those features to learned radial weights that smoothly reach zero at the maximum distance.

Spherical harmonics

Functions of a unit direction that provide angular features with known rotation and reflection behavior.

Neighbor pooling

A reduction over incoming neighbor contributions whose result does not depend on neighbor order. MACE sums the directed edge contributions for each receiving atom.

Tensor product

An operation that couples geometric features into features with prescribed symmetry. MACE uses it on edges and again on pooled node features.

Node features

Learned per-atom quantities carried between message-passing layers. Their components follow specified geometric transformation rules.

Readout

A learned map from node channels that stay fixed under rotations and reflections to one scalar energy contribution for an atom.

MACE first computes relative geometry on every directed edge. The radial basis encodes distance, while spherical harmonics encode direction. An edgewise tensor product couples those geometric features with the sending atom’s features. Neighbor pooling sums the edge contributions into single-neighbor features of each receiving atom. Symmetrized tensor products of those pooled features create multi-neighbor features. A learned linear expansion forms the message, the update produces new node features, and the readout selects rotation-fixed channels for one atomic energy contribution. The model sums those contributions and differentiates the total energy with respect to positions. This order follows the paper’s message construction, update, and readout phases.[1]

Two different orders: The graph does not assign a sequence to neighbors. Neighbor pooling removes neighbor ordering. The computation still has a stage order: edge geometry must exist before edge tensor products, and pooled node features must exist before multi-neighbor tensor products.
Part 2

The graph and physical targets

A maximum distance turns coordinates into local neighborhoods. One scalar contribution per atom produces a global energy, whose position derivative supplies a vector for each atom.

Cutoff radius

The maximum edge distance. Atom j belongs to atom i’s neighborhood when their distance is below this radius.

Site energy

A learned scalar contribution assigned to one atom’s local environment. The model sums all site energies to predict the structure’s total potential energy.

Force

The negative derivative of total potential energy with respect to an atomic position. Each atom therefore receives a three-component vector.

Four atoms, one local environment

Place carbon C₀ at the origin. Three atoms lie at distances 1.1 Å, 1.4 Å, and 1.8 Å. With a 2.0 Å cutoff, all three connect to C₀. An atom at 2.3 Å does not.

Suppose the model assigns site energies −1.2, −0.7, −0.4, and +0.1 eV to the four atoms. The total is −2.2 eV.

Now move C₀ by +0.01 Å along x. If the predicted total energy rises by about 0.003 eV, its local slope is about +0.30 eV/Å. The corresponding force component points in the opposite direction and is about −0.30 eV/Å.

The same relation in symbols is

E = Σi Ei     and     Fi = −∂E / ∂ri.

The readout predicts scalar site energies, rather than independent force vectors. Differentiation ties every force component to one scalar energy surface. The paper defines the node-level target as site energy and restricts readout to node channels that stay fixed under rotations and reflections.[1]

Planted fact: Every edge has two geometric parts: the scalar distance and the unit direction. Part 5 maps them to the radial basis and spherical harmonics.
Common trap: A site energy is not a separately observed label for each atom. It is an internal decomposition whose sum matches the structure-level energy.
Part 3

Features under rotations and reflections

Energy keeps its value when the structure rotates or reflects. Directional internal quantities follow the same geometric operation.

Orthogonal group O(3)

The three-dimensional rotations and reflections represented by orthogonal matrices Q. MACE organizes its geometric features under this group.

Invariant feature

A quantity that remains unchanged under the chosen transformation. Distance, total energy, and site energy are invariant under O(3).

O(3)-equivariant feature

A feature that transforms in a prescribed way when the input rotates or reflects. A vector-like channel rotates or reflects with the structure.

Symmetry order L

The index that labels an O(3) transformation type in the paper. L = 0 gives invariant scalars; L > 0 gives equivariant features with 2L + 1 components.

A 90° rotation

An edge of length 1.4 Å points along (1, 0, 0). Rotate the whole environment by 90° around z. The length remains 1.4 Å, while the direction becomes (0, 1, 0).

A scalar radial feature with value 0.55 remains 0.55. A vector-like feature (2, 0, 0) becomes (0, 2, 0). The total energy remains the same, and each force vector rotates with its atom.

For a geometric transformation Q ∈ O(3), invariance and equivariance read

scalar(Qr) = scalar(r)     and     hL(Qr) = DL(Q) hL(r).

DL(Q) applies the prescribed transformation to the components of an order-L feature. Spherical harmonics provide direction-dependent channels that obey these transformation rules. Tensor products then couple compatible channels without breaking equivariance. The final readout uses only L = 0 channels, so every predicted site energy stays invariant.[1]

Common trap: Equivariance does not mean that every internal number stays fixed. It means each feature follows its assigned transformation law. Only invariant channels remain numerically unchanged.
Part 4

Combining several neighbors

MACE first pools one-neighbor contributions, then multiplies the pooled features to represent joint dependence on several neighbors.

Body order

The number of atom states that a message term depends on jointly. A central atom plus one, two, or three neighbors gives two-, three-, or four-body state dependence.

Two-body message

A message term that depends on the receiving atom and one neighboring atom. In the first MACE construction, each edge contributes a two-body feature before pooling.

A-features

The equivariant two-body features produced by summing edgewise tensor-product contributions over the neighbors of a receiving atom. Their value does not depend on neighbor order.

Correlation order ν

The maximum number of pooled A-feature factors coupled in the higher-order product. Relative to atom states, correlation order ν corresponds to message body order ν + 1.

B-features

The higher-order equivariant features constructed by symmetric tensor products of the pooled A-features.

Receptive field

The spatial region whose input can affect a node feature. Stacking local message-passing layers expands this region across successive neighborhoods.

Three edge contributions become many-neighbor terms

Take one scalar slice of the three incoming edge contributions and assign values 1, 2, and 3. Neighbor pooling gives A = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6.

At correlation order 2, the product is A² = 36. Expanding that square yields nine ordered pair terms: 1×1, 1×2, through 3×3. Thus one product of the pooled feature contains all neighbor-pair contributions, including repeated neighbors.

At correlation order 3, A³ = 216 contains 27 ordered triple terms. The actual model performs symmetry-aware tensor products across all relevant channels, rather than this one scalar multiplication.

After the numerical expansion, the symbolic factorization is direct:

Ai = Σj∈N(i) aji   ⇒   Ai⊗ν = Σj₁,…,jν aj₁i ⊗ ··· ⊗ ajνi.

MACE symmetrizes and couples these products to obtain the higher-order B-features. The reference implementation names this operation SymmetricContractionWrapper inside EquivariantProductBasisBlock.[1][3]

Why higher-order messages need fewer layers

Two-body constructionOne layer directly relates the receiver to one sender at a time. Successive updates can combine information that traveled through more nodes, but each layer also expands the receptive field.
Higher-order constructionOne layer directly includes products involving several neighbors of the same receiver. The layer gains local many-body dependence without adding another spatial hop.

A network therefore does not need extra layers solely to manufacture these local joint interactions through repeated two-body updates. Higher-order messages separate increased local body order from increased receptive field. This mechanism explains why MACE can use a shallow stack. The exact number of useful layers still depends on the spatial range required by the system.[1]

Common trap: Correlation order is not layer count. ν = 3 means three pooled factors in one local construction, while three layers mean three successive rounds of information flow.
Part 5

The first MACE message construction

The planted distance-direction split now becomes the radial and angular edge representation. A one-channel trace shows where each operation acts.

Clebsch-Gordan coefficients

Fixed coupling coefficients that select how input symmetry types combine into an output symmetry type. Their generalized form symmetrizes the higher-order MACE products.

One illustrative channel for C₀
  1. Radial stage. The distances 1.1, 1.4, and 1.8 Å enter a radial basis and radial network. Suppose one resulting radial channel gives 0.80, 0.55, and 0.20.
  2. Angular stage. The three unit directions enter one selected spherical-harmonic channel. Suppose its values are +1.0, 0.0, and −0.6.
  3. Edge tensor product. Use one illustrative element channel with H = 0.5 and O = 1.2. The three edge contributions in this slice are 0.80×1.0×0.5 = 0.40, 0.55×0.0×1.2 = 0, and 0.20×(−0.6)×0.5 = −0.06.
  4. Neighbor pooling. The sum gives one A-feature slice: 0.40 + 0 − 0.06 = 0.34.
  5. Higher-order tensor products. A scalar illustration at ν = 2 gives B₂ = 0.34² = 0.1156. Full MACE couples all permitted feature components using generalized Clebsch-Gordan coefficients.
  6. Message and update. Suppose a learned linear combination gives m = 1.5×0.34 − 0.8×0.1156 = 0.4175. A linear update combines this message with the previous node features.
  7. Scalar readout. The readout maps the updated invariant channels to one site-energy contribution. Directional channels can influence that scalar through valid tensor couplings, but the output itself has L = 0.

The first-layer paper equation compresses stages 1 through 4:

Ai,klm(1) = Σj∈N(i) Rkl(1)(rji) Ylm(r̂ji) Wkzⱼ(1).

R supplies learned radial weights, Y supplies the direction-dependent spherical-harmonic component, and W embeds the sending element. The sum over j performs neighbor pooling. The paper then forms B-features from symmetrized tensor products of A, takes a learned linear expansion over correlation orders to form the message, applies a linear update, and reads site energy from invariant channels.[1]

The reference code preserves the same conceptual boundaries: RadialEmbeddingBlock builds radial edge features, RealAgnosticInteractionBlock applies a radial network, an edge tensor product, and scatter_sum, EquivariantProductBasisBlock invokes SymmetricContractionWrapper, and LinearReadoutBlock maps node features to scalars.[3] The official README describes MACE as higher-order equivariant message passing and uses invariant and higher-order feature specifications in its model interface.[2]

Common traps: Neighbor pooling does not discard direction because it sums equivariant channels, not distances alone. The higher-order tensor product occurs after pooling on each node. The readout does not convert an arbitrary vector component directly into energy; it maps invariant channels to a scalar.

Recap grid

Atomistic graph

Atoms are nodes; cutoff-limited directed edges carry relative distance and direction.

Local environment

One receiver and its in-cutoff neighbors determine a local message construction.

Site energy

An invariant per-atom contribution. Summing all contributions gives total energy.

Force

The negative position derivative of total energy, with three components per atom.

Invariant feature

Its value stays fixed under O(3), as distance and energy do.

O(3)-equivariant feature

Its components rotate or reflect according to a specified transformation law.

A-features

Neighbor-pooled two-body features that retain equivariant directional information.

Correlation order ν

The number of pooled factors in a higher-order product; message body order is ν + 1.

Scalar readout

A learned map from invariant node channels to one site-energy contribution.
